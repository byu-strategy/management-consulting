Severity was calibrated to proposal conventions, not slide conventions: long prose paragraphs are normal in this format and not flagged as "text-heavy body slide."

## Structure

Proposal opens with McKinsey's cover letter (PDF p.5), TOC (p.6), then a one-page executive summary (p.7). Body is organized around three phases of work: Phase 1 assess current state, Phase 2 design future operating structures, Phase 3 plan implementation and budgeting (pp.8-12). A project timeline section follows with a key-dates table and a 24-week Gantt (pp.13-14). A Qualifications section argues five reasons McKinsey is the right vendor (pp.15-16). An Experience section shows case examples on org design in military context and org separations (pp.17-18). Key Personnel names the proposed team with bios (pp.19-21). Pricing gives a firm fixed price of $3,994,610 with a rate-card exhibit (p.22). Assumptions and Disclosure pages follow (pp.23-24). Appendix A is the full resume set.

## Slide-by-slide

### Page 1 (PDF p.4) — Title / cover
**Title:** "Enabling organizational effectiveness of Military Affairs and Veterans Services"
**Role:** Open the proposal; signal McKinsey brand; identify the recipient and the bid.

**Issues:**
- [3] Title is a gerund phrase, not a commitment. "Enabling organizational effectiveness" is the client's aspiration, not a claim about what McKinsey will produce. A sharper cover would say what the reader gets ("Standing up two cabinet agencies in 24 weeks") or what McKinsey will prove ("A 24-week path to separate DMAVA")
- [3] Dense legal disclaimer in small italic at bottom of cover is standard but crowds the cover's airy design

**Take:** Handsome McKinsey-standard cover with the blue ray motif; the headline describes the topic rather than staking a position.

**Role:** Map the document's nine sections for the reader.

**Issues:**
- None.

**Take:** Clean, conventional, correctly paginated.

### Page 1 (PDF p.7) — Executive summary
**Title:** "Executive Summary"
**Role:** Compress the ask, the scope, and the case for McKinsey into one page.

**Issues:**
- [2] Topic-label title ("Executive Summary"). Real exec-summary convention here would be acceptable; a sharper move is a headline that commits ("McKinsey will stand up two cabinet agencies in 24 weeks for $4.0M, drawing on 10+ years of NJ service and 600+ veterans on staff"). The summary does not open with an answer-first headline
- [2] Paragraph three opens with credentials ("20,000 consultants... 5,460 engagements") before naming a single insight about DMAVA. The exec summary tells the reader who McKinsey is before telling them what McKinsey will do. A client reading only this page learns firm scale but not the proposed approach
- [3] The second paragraph lists scope in one run-on sentence (analyses of current operating structure; best practices; design; cost identification; transition plan). The reader has to reparse to recover structure
- [3] Pop-culture phrase "impeccable track record" appears later in the doc and is not exec-summary prose; the exec summary itself is measured

**Take:** A functioning exec summary that establishes scope and scale, but the one page that matters most on a $4M pitch is doing credentialing work before it does argumentative work.

---

### Pages 2-3 (PDF p.8-9) — Scope of Work, Phase 1
**Title:** "Scope of work" / "Phase 1: Assess current state of DMAVA's structure, processes, and context"
**Role:** Define what McKinsey does in the first 8 weeks; enumerate activities and deliverables.

**Issues:**
- [3] Activity 1 pairs a defined term ("North Star") with a definition in parentheses, which is crisp, but activities 2-6 vary in structure: some start with an imperative ("Map", "Identify"), some read like topic statements. The list is not strictly parallel
- [3] Activity 5 is a single sentence that runs over ten lines; activity 6 similarly long. Denser bullets in a proposal are fine, but these two swallow their own structure
- [3] "Deliverable 1c" referenced in the deliverables list but the activity map does not use this numbering scheme; a reader reconciling activities to deliverables has to infer

**Take:** Comprehensive scope, faithfully anchored to the RFQ's purposes; the internal numbering and parallelism would reward a final editing pass.

### Page 4 (PDF p.10, exhibit only) — Exhibit 1: Organizational Design Framework
**Title:** "McKinsey's Organizational Design Framework"
**Role:** Show a methodology anchor that organizes Phase 2.

**Issues:**
- [2] The wheel diagram is a standalone graphic with no takeaway caption. It names the framework ("Processes / Structure / People / Strategy at center") but does not state what the framework does for DMAVA. A consulting exhibit labels the "so what" alongside the visual
- [3] The wheel appears mid-page between two phase blocks, so its relationship to Phase 2 vs Phase 1 is visually ambiguous; the reader infers from position

**Take:** Canonical McKinsey org-design wheel used as decorative anchor; it would earn its place with one line explaining how this specific frame will be applied to DMAVA.

### Page 8 (PDF p.14) — Exhibit 3: Gantt
**Title:** "Workplan and timeline for completion of activities"
**Role:** Show the 24-week work plan visually.

**Issues:**
- [3] The Gantt shows bar positions but not bar shading for intensity; it is binary (active or not active), so a client asking "how many consultants in what week" cannot read it off the chart
- [3] Row 5 "Contract Management" runs all 24 weeks with weekly diamond markers; this is operationally accurate but visually dominant and risks misleading the eye about where real work happens

**Take:** A genuine 24-week plan rendered readably; minor craft marks rather than substantive flaws.

### Pages 9-10 (PDF p.15-16) — Qualifications
**Title:** "Qualifications"
**Role:** Argue why McKinsey beats other bidders.

**Issues:**
- [2] Qualification 1 headline reads "More than 20-times greater breadth and depth of experience serving State of New Jersey compared with other eligible bidders." This is an uncited comparative claim against unnamed competitors. "20x" is a specific numeric assertion with no derivation (50 projects divided by what denominator?). Either show the comparator or soften the headline
- [2] Qualification 2 uses the word "impeccable" to describe track record. Self-awarded superlatives without a citation weaken rather than strengthen; clients register this as sales copy
- [3] Qualifications 2 and 3 both lead with "Other eligible bidders may only have..." The rhetorical scaffold repeats
- [3] Page 10 has two large black redaction blocks inside qualification 4 covering what appears to be a major client reference; the redactions are operationally required but create a visual void readers notice

**Take:** Genuine differentiators (NJ depth, 600+ veterans on staff, 80+ years serving veterans) exist in this section, but they are prefaced with overclaimed comparatives that draw an experienced reader's eye before the evidence does.

---

### Pages 11-12 (PDF p.17-18) — Experience on Similar Projects
**Title:** "Experience on Similar Projects" / "Case examples on Organizational Design in Military Context" / "Case examples on Organizational Separations"
**Role:** Prove the firm has done this work before via case summaries.

**Issues:**
- [1] Cases 1, 2, 3, and 4 are entirely redacted. The visible summaries consist of the case number and the client industry only; "Summary" and "Impact" sections are black bars. For a proposal that leads its qualifications with "unrivaled expertise in organizational redesign including separations," four of seven most-relevant cases being unreadable is a glaring gap. The client cannot evaluate fit on the most relevant comparators
- [2] The case that most closely matches DMAVA's situation (Case 5: Large Midwestern State, Department of Children and Youth) is the one exposed example of a state-agency creation. It is a strong analog. But it is one case; three of the four readable cases are private-sector separations (technology, oil and gas), which are less apposite
- [3] Case 5 summary uses "~10" for cabinet-level departments; earlier sections of the proposal use specific counts. Precision varies

**Take:** The one visible on-point case is well-matched; the redaction of the most relevant military-context cases is structurally required for the client's own confidentiality reasons, but it leaves the qualification claim under-evidenced from the reader's perspective.

### Pages 13-15 (PDF p.19-21) — Key Personnel
**Title:** "Key Personnel"
**Role:** Name the team, argue individual fit.

**Issues:**
- [3] The lead partner paragraph (Scott Blackburn) ends with "In 2018 Scott was recognized by the National Association of State Directors of Veterans Affairs (NASDVA) as Veteran Advocate of the Year" with a missing period. One small punctuation break
- [3] Matt Watters bio includes "Distinctive people leader" as a self-assessment phrase; later bios use similar self-descriptors ("distinctive", "deep-felt personal obligation"). A careful editor would swap these for achievements
- [3] "Examples of representative full team members are provided below. If McKinsey is selected for this project, we will determine the availability of these colleagues for staffing..." is a standard disclaimer but reveals that the named full-team consultants (Alison Wagner, Quinton Allen, Kelsey Price, Taylor Ourada) may not actually be on the engagement. A procurement reader notices

**Take:** Team is credible and substantively right for the work (veterans, NJ state experience, org-design and separations SMEs, a NASA precedent); the writing leans on self-description where concrete achievements would do the work.

### Page 16 (PDF p.22) — Pricing

**Issues:**

### Pages 17-18 (PDF p.23-24) — Assumptions and Disclosure
**Title:** "Assumptions and Disclosure"
**Role:** Govern the commercial terms; disclose potential conflicts.

**Issues:**
- [2] Assumption 6 states: "McKinsey does not provide categorical recommendations on matters of public policy; but rather, provides fact-based analysis and framing of options and tradeoffs." The RFQ's section II explicitly asks for "recommendations for the structure, functions, and personnel" of two new cabinet-level agencies. The assumption and the ask sit in tension; a reader could conclude McKinsey is pre-reserving against what the client is paying for. If this is a reasonable boundary (analysis yes, formal policy recommendations no), it should be reconciled with the scope section, not only asserted here
- [3] Assumption 7 (McKinsey US affiliate conflict list) is almost a full page of industry enumeration; necessary for disclosure, but it dominates the Assumptions section visually

**Take:** The disclosure content is required and well-drafted, but Assumption 6 surfaces a tension with the scope that should be addressed rather than absorbed silently.

**Take:** These are all topic-label section headers, which is the proposal convention (not a slide convention). Read as a doc TOC they are coherent and complete. A slide deck with these headers would be heavily flagged for topic-label titles; in a Word proposal this is normal. Flagging it would mis-apply the rubric, so I do not ding it.

**Template saturation:**

Low to moderate. The three phases use parallel "Key activities / Key deliverables" sub-headings, which is appropriate for a scope document. Qualifications 1-5 all open with "More than..." or "Other eligible bidders may only have..." (qualifications 1, 2, 3), showing a formulaic rhetorical scaffold across the firm's differentiators. Case examples use a strict "Summary / Impact" two-field template across all seven cases. Bios open with "X is a [title] in McKinsey's [office]..." uniformly. This is McKinsey house style, not AI-generated template saturation. No "Move 01 / 02 / 03" or "Why It Works / What It Delivers / Risk" patterns.

**Inconsistencies:**

Deliverable numbering skips from 1a-1c to 7b-7c without an explicit 2-6 or 7a anchor. Bullet punctuation varies (some lists end sentences, some do not). "State of New Jersey" is capitalized consistently but "the State" vs "the state" alternates in lowercase text. The rate-card table has extra intra-cell spacing on two rows. Case 5 uses "~10" while most other counts in the doc are exact. Running header uses "Department of Military and Veterans Affairs" while the body sometimes writes "Department of Military and Veterans' Affairs" (apostrophe) and sometimes "Department of Military and Veteran Services" (singular). These are proofreading artifacts, not substantive errors.

**Generic applicability:**

The proposal substantially passes the swap test on scope (phases are generic McKinsey org-design phases that could be applied to any state agency separation). It substantially fails the swap test on evidence, which is the right direction: Qualification 1 names specific NJ agencies (Banking and Insurance, Children and Families, Health, Human Services, Treasury, NJEDA, NJ Transit, Office of Emergency Management, DEI, IT); Case 5 names "Large Midwestern State, Department of Children and Youth" with the 10-agency specific structure; Scott Blackburn's VA credentials are specific and checkable (MyVA Taskforce lead 2014-2017, VA CIO, NASDVA Veteran Advocate of the Year 2018). Team has a NJ-native partner (Matt Watters). The exec summary still leans on generic scale claims (20,000 consultants, 5,460 engagements, 105+ countries) before it makes the NJ-specific argument, so the "generic before specific" sequencing is where the template risk actually lives.

## Overall take

As a proposal document this is a legitimate and competent McKinsey response: clear three-phase scope, a real 24-week Gantt with deliverable dates, a transparent firm-fixed-price breakdown, a veteran-credentialed lead partner, and a NJ-native partner on the team. The biggest tell of a bid that could be sharper is the gap between the qualification claims ("20-times greater breadth," "impeccable track record," "unrivaled expertise in organizational redesign including separations") and the evidence actually visible in the Experience section, where four of the seven most-relevant cases are fully redacted. The single change that would raise this proposal the most is rewriting the executive summary answer-first, leading with the specific DMAVA commitment ("stand up two cabinet agencies in 24 weeks for $4.0M") and the one or two most on-point proof points (Case 5 and Scott Blackburn's VA record), before ever mentioning firm-scale statistics.
