# Structuring Cases with Two Kinds of MECE

*A student-facing guide to case interview structuring using the accounting MECE / coverage MECE distinction*

---

## The One Thing Most Case Prep Gets Wrong

Every case prep resource teaches you to "be MECE." None of them tells you that MECE means two different things, and the difference determines whether your structure is genuinely rigorous or just organized brainstorming.

**Accounting MECE** partitions a quantity. Every dollar, every customer, every unit lands in exactly one bucket. The buckets sum to a verifiable total. If they don't, you have an error. This is mathematical.

**Coverage MECE** partitions a space of ideas. You're surveying possible causes, options, or evaluation criteria. Nothing is being summed. The goal is completeness -- don't miss anything, don't list the same thing twice. This is a checklist.

Every case interview involves both types. The best candidates know which one they're doing at each moment and signal the transition clearly. The weakest candidates do coverage MECE while thinking they're doing accounting MECE -- producing clean-looking structures that don't actually prove anything.

---

## How This Maps to Frameworks You Already Know

If you've used Victor Cheng's *Case Interview Secrets* or his Core Frameworks, you already know the two types -- you just don't have names for them yet.

**Cheng's Profitability Framework** is pure accounting MECE. Profit = Revenue - Cost. Revenue = Revenue/Unit x Units Sold. Cost = Fixed + Variable. Every branch decomposes algebraically. The math must add up. His advice on this framework -- "SEGMENT the number, break it up into its component parts, compare to historical metrics to find where the shift is" -- is accounting MECE doctrine. His repeated insistence to "ALWAYS, ALWAYS SEGMENT YOUR NUMBERS" is the partition-choice principle: try multiple segmentations (by product, channel, customer type, region) and look for where the variance lives.

**Cheng's Business Situation Framework** (Customer, Product, Company, Competition) is pure coverage MECE. There's no quantity being summed across these four buckets. Customer insights + Product analysis + Company capabilities + Competitive landscape does not equal anything. It's a checklist of lenses to ensure you've examined the full strategic space. He applies this framework to market entry, new product, growth strategy, turnarounds, and company position assessments -- all cases where the structuring task is conceptual, not mathematical.

**The transition Cheng describes but doesn't name:** On his profitability framework page, he writes: "Once you know mathematically what's causing the problem, you need to understand WHY the number has declined in the context of the marketplace." He calls this a "compound framework" -- using the profitability tree first, then the business situation framework. That's accounting MECE first (find *where*), then coverage MECE (understand *why*). He's teaching the same transition this guide names explicitly.

**Cheng's M&A "Fit" Framework** applies the Business Situation Framework (coverage MECE) to Company A, Company B, and Company A+B, then looks for synergies. The synergy identification is coverage MECE. The valuation math that accompanies it is accounting MECE.

**Cheng's Capacity Change Framework** (Demand, Supply, Cost of Expansion) is mixed. Demand and supply analysis involves quantities (units, prices, curves) -- accounting MECE. The strategic decision of whether to expand is coverage MECE (weighing costs, alternatives, risks).

The same mapping works for other prep resources. CaseCoach's frameworks, PrepLounge's case types, and Hacking the Case Interview's 14 types all mix accounting and coverage MECE without distinguishing them. The vocabulary in this guide lets you see which type each framework is doing -- and, critically, when a case requires you to shift from one to the other.

## The Case Type Map

Case interviews cluster into fourteen types. Each type has a characteristic structure that involves both accounting and coverage MECE at different moments.

### 1. Profitability

**The prompt:** "Our client's profits have declined 15% year-over-year. Why, and what should they do?"

**Subtypes:** Profit decline (margin compression), cost problem (costs rising faster than revenue), revenue problem (revenue declining or flat), scenario comparison (which of two strategies is more profitable)

**The accounting MECE move:** Start here. Profit = Revenue - Cost. Revenue = Price x Volume. Cost = Fixed + Variable. This is a mathematical identity -- every dollar of profit change lands somewhere in this tree. Drill into whichever branch is driving the change.

**The coverage MECE move:** Once you've identified *where* the problem is (e.g., "variable costs are up 20%"), shift to *why*. Possible drivers of variable cost increase: input price inflation, supplier contract changes, product mix shift toward higher-cost items, waste/inefficiency, volume-driven diseconomies. These are hypotheses to test, not quantities to sum.

**The transition signal:** "The math tells us that variable costs are the primary driver of the profit decline. Now I'd like to explore the possible root causes to identify which ones are actionable."

**Common error:** Jumping straight to coverage MECE ("I'll look at revenue, costs, market dynamics, and competitive factors") without first doing the accounting decomposition that tells you where the problem actually lives. This wastes time investigating branches that aren't broken.

### 2. Revenue Growth

**The prompt:** "How can our client grow revenue by 20% over the next three years?"

**The accounting MECE move:** Revenue growth = growth from existing customers (retention improvement + upsell/cross-sell + price increases) + growth from new customers (organic acquisition + new segments + new geographies) + growth from inorganic sources (acquisitions, partnerships). Every dollar of growth falls in one bucket.

**The coverage MECE move:** For each growth bucket, what are the available levers? For new customer acquisition: channel expansion, marketing investment, product modifications for new segments, geographic entry. These are strategic options, not quantities.

**The transition signal:** "Let me first size each growth pathway to see which can realistically contribute to the 20% target, then explore the specific levers within the most promising pathways."

**Common error:** Listing growth ideas without sizing them. "We could enter new markets, launch new products, and increase prices" is coverage MECE -- but without accounting MECE to estimate the contribution of each, you can't assess whether they add up to 20%.

---

### 3. Cost Reduction / Operations

**The prompt:** "Find $50M in annual cost savings without impacting quality."

**The accounting MECE move:** Total cost = personnel + materials/COGS + technology + facilities + third-party services + other. Decompose actual spend by category. Identify which categories are largest and which have grown fastest.

**The coverage MECE move:** For each major cost category, what reduction levers exist? Three universal levers: demand reduction (do less of it), supply optimization (do it more efficiently), rate reduction (pay less for it). Each lever is conceptually distinct.

**The transition signal:** "Labor represents 55% of our cost base and has grown 12% year-over-year -- it's the largest and fastest-growing category. Let me now think about what's driving that increase and which levers are available."

**Common error:** Mixing cost categories (accounting) with cost drivers (coverage) in the same structure. "Labor costs, supplier pricing, operational inefficiency, and rent" -- "operational inefficiency" is a cause, not a cost line item, and it could affect any of the other three.

---

### 4. Market Entry

**The prompt:** "Should our client enter the Indian market for electric vehicles?"

**The accounting MECE move:** Market sizing. TAM = number of potential buyers x average revenue per buyer. SAM = TAM filtered by segments your product can serve. SOM = SAM x realistic capture rate. Each customer is counted once; the levels are nested subsets.

**The coverage MECE move:** The go/no-go evaluation. Market attractiveness (size, growth, profitability, competitive intensity), competitive dynamics (number of players, barriers to entry, differentiation potential, substitutes), internal readiness (capability gaps, investment required, organizational fit, distribution access), and risk profile (regulatory, execution, market timing, political).

**The transition signal:** "Let me first size the opportunity to see if it's worth pursuing, then evaluate whether we can win in this market."

**Common error:** Treating the evaluation criteria as if they were additive. "Market attractiveness: 8/10, competitive position: 6/10, internal readiness: 7/10, average = 7/10, so we should enter." These scores don't sum or average in any meaningful way. A market can be attractive (large, growing) AND unwinnable (entrenched competitors, regulatory barriers). The interaction between criteria matters more than any individual score.

---

### 5. M&A and Investment

**The prompt:** "Should our client acquire Company X?" or "Should this PE fund invest in Company Y?"

**Subtypes:** Strategic buyer (acquiring for capabilities, market position, or synergies) vs. financial buyer (acquiring for return on investment, value creation through operational improvement)

**The accounting MECE move:** Valuation. For a strategic buyer: standalone value + synergy value - integration costs = deal value. For a financial buyer: entry multiple x EBITDA, value creation through revenue growth + margin expansion + debt paydown + multiple expansion, exit value at target year. Every dollar of value creation falls in one bucket.

**The coverage MECE move:** Strategic rationale (why this target, why now, what alternatives exist), due diligence risks (market risks, operational risks, financial risks, legal/regulatory risks, integration risks), and synergy assessment (revenue synergies, cost synergies, capability synergies -- and which are achievable vs. aspirational).

**The transition signal:** "The financial case shows this deal creates $200M in value at our base case. Now let me assess whether the strategic rationale is sound and what risks could erode that value."

**Common error:** For financial buyers, forgetting that value creation levers are multiplicative, not additive. Revenue growth x margin expansion x multiple expansion compounds -- a 10% improvement on each doesn't produce 30% more value, it produces ~33%. For strategic buyers, double-counting synergies that appear in both "revenue synergy" and "cost synergy" categories.

---

### 6. Market Sizing / Estimation

**The prompt:** "How many electric vehicle charging stations will the US need by 2030?" or "What is the market size for premium pet food in Europe?"

**The accounting MECE move:** This case type is almost entirely accounting MECE. You're building a calculation tree where every unit is counted exactly once.

Top-down approach: Start with a large known number and filter down. US population → car owners → EV owners by 2030 → charging needs per owner → stations needed.

Bottom-up approach: Start with unit economics and build up. Number of metro areas x stations per metro area x average utilization x revenue per charge = market revenue.

**The coverage MECE move:** Minimal, but it appears in two places: (1) identifying which segments to include in your calculation (what counts as "premium" pet food? dry, wet, treats, supplements?), and (2) pressure-testing your assumptions (what could make this estimate wrong? adoption rate assumptions, technology changes, regulatory shifts, competitive dynamics).

**The transition signal:** "Let me walk you through my calculation structure, and then I'll pressure-test the key assumptions."

**Common error:** Not being explicit about your segmentation choices. If "premium pet food" includes treats and supplements in your calculation but the interviewer is thinking only about main meals, your number will be off and the disagreement is about scope definition (a coverage MECE issue), not math.

---

### 7. Pricing

**The prompt:** "How should our client price this new product?" or "Should we raise prices on our existing portfolio?"

**The accounting MECE move:** Unit economics. Revenue per unit = price. Cost per unit = variable cost + allocated fixed cost. Margin per unit = price - cost per unit. Break-even volume = fixed costs / margin per unit. Price-volume sensitivity: if we raise price 10%, how much volume can we lose before profit declines?

**The coverage MECE move:** Pricing strategy evaluation. Three lenses: cost-based (what's our floor?), competition-based (where do competitors price? what's the reference range?), value-based (what's the customer's willingness to pay? what value do we deliver relative to alternatives?). Then pricing model: flat fee, per-unit, subscription, tiered, freemium, usage-based. Each model has different implications for customer acquisition, lifetime value, and competitive positioning.

**The transition signal:** "Let me anchor on unit economics to establish the price floor, then evaluate where we should price relative to competitors and customer willingness to pay."

**Common error:** Optimizing price in isolation from volume. Price and volume are multiplicatively linked (Revenue = P x Q), and a price increase that looks good in a spreadsheet may destroy volume in the market. This is the accounting MECE trap of treating multiplicative factors as if they're independent.

---

### 8. New Product / Launch Strategy

**The prompt:** "Our client is considering launching a new product line. Should they proceed, and how?"

**The accounting MECE move:** Business case. Development cost + launch cost + ongoing operating cost vs. projected revenue over the planning horizon. NPV or payback period calculation. Revenue projection = addressable market x capture rate x price.

**The coverage MECE move:** The should-we/how-should-we evaluation. Customer need validation (is there a real unmet need? how strong is the evidence?), product-market fit (does our concept address the need better than alternatives?), competitive response (how will incumbents react?), go-to-market (how do we reach the target customer?), internal capability (can we build and deliver this?), risks and unknowns.

**The transition signal:** "Let me first validate whether the economics work at a high level, then evaluate the strategic factors that determine whether this is a good bet."

**Common error:** Falling in love with the product concept and skipping the business case math. The accounting MECE forces you to answer "even if everything goes well, does this make enough money to justify the investment?" before spending time on the coverage MECE of how to execute.

---

### 9. Organizational / People

**The prompt:** "Our client is experiencing 30% annual employee turnover. Diagnose the problem and recommend solutions."

**The accounting MECE move:** Decompose the turnover. By department, by tenure cohort, by performance rating, by geography. Every departing employee falls in one bucket. Look for where turnover is concentrated -- is it uniform or driven by a specific segment?

**The coverage MECE move:** Drivers of turnover. Push factors (compensation, manager quality, career development, workload, culture) vs. pull factors (competitor offers, industry demand shifts, remote work preferences). Within push factors, what does exit data and engagement survey data reveal?

**The transition signal:** "Turnover is concentrated in the engineering function -- 45% annual turnover versus 20% company-wide. Let me now explore what's driving engineers specifically to leave."

**Common error:** Jumping to solutions ("we need to raise salaries") before diagnosing who is leaving and why. The accounting decomposition narrows the problem; the coverage decomposition identifies the cause; only then can you design a targeted solution.

---

### 10. Competitive Response

**The prompt:** "A low-cost competitor has entered our client's core market and is gaining share rapidly. How should we respond?"

**The accounting MECE move:** Quantify the threat. Market share over time: our share vs. competitor's share vs. rest of market. Revenue at risk by segment, product line, or customer type. Price gap analysis: their price vs. ours, by product tier.

**The coverage MECE move:** Response options. Defend (match pricing, increase loyalty programs, lock in contracts), differentiate (invest in features/quality/service the competitor can't match), attack (enter their stronghold segments, launch a fighter brand), or concede (exit low-margin segments, redirect resources to defensible positions). Then evaluate each option against feasibility, cost, and likely competitor counter-response.

**The transition signal:** "They've captured 8% share in 18 months, concentrated in our mid-tier segment. Let me evaluate our response options for that segment specifically."

**Common error:** Defaulting to "match their price" without quantifying the margin impact. The accounting MECE (what does a 10% price cut do to our P&L across all segments?) often reveals that matching is more expensive than it appears, which opens up the coverage MECE of alternative responses.

---

### 11. Valuation

**The prompt:** "What is Company X worth?" or "Our PE client is evaluating a target at 8x EBITDA -- is that a fair price?"

This sometimes appears within M&A cases but also stands alone, especially in private equity and investment-focused interviews.

**The accounting MECE move:** This is almost entirely accounting MECE. DCF approach: project free cash flows (revenue growth, margin trajectory, capex, working capital) and discount to present value. Comparable approach: identify comparable companies or transactions, select the right multiple (EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, P/E), and apply. The components of each approach are additive and verifiable.

**The coverage MECE move:** Relatively limited. It appears in selecting which valuation methodology to use and why, identifying the key assumptions that most affect the output (coverage of sensitivity drivers), and evaluating strategic premium or discount factors that the math alone doesn't capture.

**The transition signal:** "My base case DCF gives a range of $400-500M. Let me now test which assumptions the valuation is most sensitive to."

**Common error:** False precision. Building a detailed DCF model and presenting a single-point valuation ("the company is worth $437M") when every input is an estimate. The accounting MECE is rigorous in structure but the inputs are uncertain -- acknowledge the range.

---

### 12. Private Equity

**The prompt:** "A PE fund is evaluating an investment in a mid-sized healthcare services company at 9x EBITDA. Should they proceed?" or "Develop a value creation plan for a portfolio company."

Private equity cases have distinct mechanics that differentiate them from strategic M&A. The buyer is a financial investor, not an operating company. The goal is return on investment over a defined hold period (typically 3-7 years), not strategic synergies.

**The accounting MECE move:** Heavy. PE cases are math-intensive. Entry valuation: EBITDA x entry multiple = enterprise value. Debt/equity split determines equity check. Value creation over the hold period decomposes into four additive buckets: revenue growth contribution + margin expansion contribution + debt paydown contribution + multiple expansion contribution = total equity value created. Exit valuation: projected EBITDA x exit multiple = exit enterprise value. Return calculation: IRR and MOIC (multiple on invested capital).

**The coverage MECE move:** Investment thesis evaluation. Market attractiveness (size, growth, resilience, fragmentation), competitive position (market share, differentiation, defensibility), value creation levers (organic growth opportunities, operational improvement potential, buy-and-build acquisition targets, management team quality), and risk assessment (customer concentration, regulatory exposure, key person dependencies, cyclicality).

**The transition signal:** "At 9x entry, we need EBITDA to grow from $50M to $80M over five years to hit a 20% IRR, assuming a 9x exit multiple and moderate debt paydown. Let me evaluate whether the value creation levers can realistically deliver that growth."

**Common error:** Treating value creation levers as independent and additive when they're multiplicative. A company that grows revenue 30% AND expands margins 500bps AND pays down debt AND exits at a higher multiple compounds these effects -- the total return is greater than the sum of the parts. Conversely, missing on any one lever can dramatically reduce returns. The accounting MECE tells you the math; understanding the interaction requires the coverage MECE lens on execution risk.

---

### 13. Public Sector / Social Impact

**The prompt:** "A city government wants to reduce homelessness by 40% in 5 years. How should they approach this?" or "A public school district needs to improve graduation rates."

These cases have different dynamics from private-sector cases: no profit motive, multiple stakeholders with conflicting objectives, political constraints, limited budget flexibility, and success metrics that are harder to quantify.

**The accounting MECE move:** Decompose the population or the budget. For homelessness: homeless population by type (chronically homeless, transitionally homeless, families, veterans, youth) -- every person in one bucket. For education: students by school, by grade, by demographic, by risk factor. Budget allocation by program type.

**The coverage MECE move:** Intervention categories. For homelessness: prevention (keep people housed), emergency response (shelters, services), transition programs (housing-first, job training, mental health), and systemic reform (zoning, affordable housing supply). For education: in-school factors (teaching quality, curriculum, support services) vs. out-of-school factors (family stability, nutrition, transportation, community safety).

**The transition signal:** "The chronically homeless are 15% of the population but consume 60% of emergency resources. Interventions targeted at this group would have the highest ROI per dollar spent."

**Common error:** Applying a private-sector profitability frame. "Maximize ROI" sounds rational but ignores equity considerations, political feasibility, and the fact that the "customers" (constituents) don't choose to be in this situation. Coverage MECE for public sector cases needs to include stakeholder alignment and implementation feasibility as explicit categories, not afterthoughts.

---

### 14. Creative / Unconventional

**The prompt:** "Our client is a national zoo. Attendance is flat. How should they think about their strategy?" or "How many piano tuners are there in Chicago?"

These cases deliberately resist standard frameworks. The interviewer is testing whether you can build structure from scratch rather than applying a memorized template. Brainteasers (estimation questions not tied to a business context) also fall here.

**The approach:** Start by clarifying the objective and constraints. Then build a custom coverage MECE that maps the problem space. For the zoo: Revenue streams (admissions, memberships, food/retail, events, sponsorships) is accounting MECE. Attendance drivers (awareness, accessibility, experience quality, repeat visit motivation, competitive alternatives) is coverage MECE. For brainteasers: build a calculation tree (accounting MECE) and be explicit about your assumptions.

**The key skill:** Being explicit about *what kind* of structure you're building and *why*. "I'm going to start by decomposing the revenue streams so we can see where the money comes from, then shift to exploring what drives attendance since that appears to be the core lever."

**Common error:** Forcing a memorized framework onto a problem it doesn't fit. The "profitability framework" applied to a zoo or a government agency will produce awkward, poorly-fitting categories. Build the structure from the problem, not from a template.

## Summary: The Case Type Map

| Case Type | Accounting MECE Moment | Coverage MECE Moment | Typical Sequencing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Profitability** | Profit = Revenue - Cost tree; identify *where* | Root cause hypotheses; identify *why* | Accounting first, then coverage |
| **Revenue Growth** | Size each growth pathway | Identify levers within each pathway | Accounting first to prioritize, then coverage |
| **Cost Reduction** | Cost breakdown by category | Reduction levers (demand, supply, rate) | Accounting first to focus, then coverage |
| **Market Entry** | Market sizing (TAM/SAM/SOM) | Go/no-go evaluation criteria | Accounting to size, coverage to decide |
| **M&A / Investment** | Valuation and value creation math | Strategic rationale and risk assessment | Both in parallel; financial and strategic cases |
| **Market Sizing** | The entire calculation tree | Assumption pressure-testing | Almost entirely accounting |
| **Pricing** | Unit economics, break-even, P x Q sensitivity | Strategy lenses (cost/competition/value), model selection | Accounting for floor, coverage for positioning |
| **New Product / Launch** | Business case (costs vs. projected revenue) | Need validation, PMF, competitive response, GTM | Accounting to sanity-check, coverage to evaluate |
| **Organizational / People** | Decompose the metric by segment | Diagnose drivers and causes | Accounting to narrow, coverage to explain |
| **Competitive Response** | Market share and revenue at risk by segment | Response options (defend, differentiate, attack, concede) | Accounting to quantify threat, coverage to evaluate options |
| **Valuation** | DCF components, comparable multiples | Methodology selection, key assumption sensitivity | Almost entirely accounting; coverage for assumptions |
| **Private Equity** | Entry valuation, value creation math (revenue + margin + debt + multiple), IRR/MOIC | Investment thesis (market, position, levers, risks) | Accounting-heavy; coverage for thesis and execution risk |
| **Public Sector / Social Impact** | Population or budget decomposition by segment | Intervention categories, stakeholder alignment | Accounting to find leverage points, coverage to design interventions |
| **Creative / Unconventional** | Decompose whatever is measurable | Build custom structure from the problem | Varies -- read the problem, don't force a template |

## Five Rules for Case Structuring

**1. Start with accounting MECE when the case gives you numbers.** It anchors your analysis, demonstrates quantitative rigor, and tells you *where* to focus before you explore *why*.

**2. Signal when you shift to coverage MECE.** "The data tells us costs are the issue. Now let me explore the possible drivers." This shows the interviewer you know the difference between identifying a problem and diagnosing its cause.

**3. Never present coverage MECE as if it's accounting MECE.** Don't assign fake percentages to qualitative factors. Don't say "I think 40% of the problem is pricing and 30% is competition." You don't know that, and the interviewer knows you don't know that.

**4. Use the Pyramid Principle within each type.** For accounting MECE, lead with the biggest bucket. For coverage MECE, lead with the most likely hypothesis. Don't bury the lead in bucket #4.

**5. Try multiple partitions mentally before committing.** Before you speak, ask yourself: "Is there a better way to cut this?" For accounting MECE, the best cut has the most variance between buckets. For coverage MECE, the best cut most directly tests your hypothesis. The first framework that comes to mind is usually the most generic. Take five seconds to consider whether a different partition would be more revealing.

## The Cheat Sheet

Print this or keep it in your head during practice:

```
Am I partitioning a QUANTITY or a SPACE OF IDEAS?

QUANTITY (Accounting MECE):
  - Every unit in one bucket
  - Buckets sum to a total
  - Check: do they add up?
  - Goal: find WHERE the problem/opportunity lives
  - Principle: seek maximum variance between buckets

SPACE OF IDEAS (Coverage MECE):
  - Surveying possible causes, options, or criteria
  - Nothing sums to anything
  - Check: did I miss anything? Any overlaps?
  - Goal: understand WHY and WHAT TO DO
  - Principle: align with your hypothesis

WITHIN A SINGLE CASE:
  Accounting MECE (where) → Coverage MECE (why) → Recommendation (what to do)
```

## Practice Drill: The Two-Sheet Method

For every practice case you do, use two sheets of paper:

**Sheet 1 (Accounting):** Write down every quantitative decomposition you used. Revenue = X. Cost = Y. Market size = Z. Check: do they add up? Where was the variance?

**Sheet 2 (Coverage):** Write down every qualitative structure you used. Root causes, evaluation criteria, strategic options. Check: any overlaps? Anything missing? Did your framing match your hypothesis?

After the case, review both sheets and ask:
- Did I start with the right type?
- Did I signal the transition clearly?
- Did I accidentally treat coverage MECE as accounting MECE (or vice versa)?
- Could a different partition on either sheet have been more revealing?

Do this for 10 cases and the distinction will become automatic.
